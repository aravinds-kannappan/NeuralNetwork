From the recorded measurements

Do the examples become more like the final model’s examples?
Higher means more similar under our text measure. The final point is 1 because it is compared with itself. We explain the measure and shaded uncertainty band below. Data: 24 poles, one training run.

Imagine watching one small part of a language model while the model learns. Early on, that part reacts strongly to one set of text snippets. Later, it reacts to different snippets. Did its role change completely, or do the new examples still have something in common with the old ones?

Abay Bektursun collected measurements that let us investigate this question. We checked calculations from his original audit, then compared the meanings of the recorded text snippets. This page explains the experiment from the beginning, shows the actual examples, and separates what we found from what we still do not know.

Start here / No background assumed

What are we actually
looking at?

A language model is a large calculation.

A language model reads text and predicts what piece of text could come next. It does this by converting the input into numbers and passing those numbers through many layers of calculations. Training changes the model’s adjustable numbers, called weights, so that its predictions improve on the training examples.

The model in the checkpoint analysis is OLMo-3 7B. “7B” is the approximate number of adjustable parameters, not the number of neurons we examined. We are studying a small selection of internal units inside that much larger model.

TextA passage enters the model→TokensThe text is split into pieces→LayersCalculations transform the input→ActivationWe record one unit’s value

A simplified explanation of what is being measured, not a complete architecture diagram. We observe internal responses while the model processes text; we are not asking it to describe its own neurons.

01

Token: a piece of text.

A token can be a word, part of a word, punctuation, or another text fragment. In the explorer, the highlighted piece is the token where the neuron’s activation was recorded. The surrounding text is its context. The highlight tells you where the measurement happened; it does not prove that this one token caused the entire response.

02

Neuron and activation: one unit, one response value.

Here, a neuron means one internal coordinate in a feed-forward part of the network, called an MLP. For each token, that coordinate produces a number: its activation. This is a mathematical unit, not a biological cell. Looking at the examples with large responses can suggest what it is sensitive to, but the neuron was not given a human-written job description.

Its activation can be positive or negative. The study examines the two directions separately and calls each direction a pole. A positive pole collects strong positive responses; a negative pole collects strong responses in the negative direction. The signs are algebraic; they do not mean positive or negative emotion. This is why 41 poles can come from 40 neurons: one underlying neuron appears in both directions.

03

Checkpoint: a saved version of the same model.

During training, the weights change. A checkpoint saves them at a particular training step, rather like a snapshot. A training step is an update using training data; it is not one word read or one sentence generated. These 16 checkpoints follow one training run from initialization to step 1,413,814. They are not 16 independently trained models.

The saved steps are unevenly spaced. Moving the explorer’s slider one notch might advance 1,000 steps early in training or hundreds of thousands later. Read the displayed step number rather than treating the slider as a uniform time scale.

04

Top contexts: the strongest examples we can see.

Abay scored a fixed corpus containing 20,560,068 tokens. The public viewer exposes the 20 strongest contexts for each selected pole at each saved checkpoint. That lets us compare what produced especially strong responses at different times.

This is a selected view, not a complete record of everything the neuron did. If a context disappears from the top 20, we do not know from this export whether it fell to rank 21 or became almost inactive. We must not fill that missing activation with zero.

05

Cluster and “job”: a group of related examples.

The original audit also used a language model to group similar activating examples into clusters and describe them. For instance, several contexts about batteries might form a group. These descriptions are hypotheses about the neuron’s behavior. A coherent description is useful, but it is not automatically a complete explanation of what the neuron computes.

The original “one job” tests use top-50 examples and additional test texts. Our new text-similarity analysis uses the released top-20 viewer. Keeping those two datasets separate prevents us from claiming that a result about one directly proves a result about the other.

Terminology follows Abay’s explanation of the measurements. The original specification and data are preserved in the repository.

What did we do with those measurements?

  1. Check the original calculations. Start from released measurements and see whether the reported counts and statistics can be obtained again. This is the reproduction.
  2. Ask an additional question. Compare the meanings of the strongest text examples across checkpoints, using a separate text-embedding model. This is the extension.
  3. Try to explain the result away. Remove repeated examples, change how text is encoded, and compare against other neurons. A result is more informative when it survives plausible alternative explanations.

Think of this as reanalyzing another researcher’s measurement files. We did not recollect all of the original measurements by running the large model ourselves.

How to read the three numbers above the examples

Semantic cosine to final
A comparison of the meanings of the two sets of text, using a separate sentence-embedding model. Higher means their summary vectors point in more similar directions. It is not the probability that the neuron has the same job. Section 03 explains how we compute it.
Exact-context Jaccard
A comparison of which exact exported contexts are shared. It is the number shared divided by the number in either set. It equals 0 when none overlap and 1 when the sets are identical. As a teaching example, two sets of 20 contexts sharing 5 have Jaccard 5 ÷ (20 + 20 − 5) = 0.143.
Largest activation magnitude
The size of the strongest response recorded for that pole at the selected checkpoint. “Magnitude” ignores the sign. A large drop is a change in response size; by itself it does not tell us whether the examples’ meanings changed.

The first two measures answer different questions and use different scales. A cosine of 0.65 beside a Jaccard of 0.13 does not by itself prove meaningful continuity. We need a fair comparison with other neurons.

02 / Reproduce

The arithmetic reproduces.
The controls still matter.

Before adding a new analysis, we checked what could be reproduced from the released files. We verified 56 input files against their SHA-256 hashes—digital fingerprints that detect changed bytes—and recomputed counts and statistics. We also reran the author’s published script for dating the appearance of example groups.

What does “one job” mean in the table?

The original audit asks whether a pole’s examples support one recognizable description that also predicts responses to new test texts. “ONE” is the name of a class assigned by that procedure. It is not a direct observation that the neuron can only ever do one thing.

The rule combines the original clustering and judgments with an explanation test. A separate model generates five texts intended to activate the pole, five close but incorrect examples, and five unrelated examples. The test requires at least three intended examples to respond strongly, at most two close misses to do so, and at most one unrelated example to do so. The released scores let us apply that rule again.

For OLMo, 311 of the 600 tested poles receive the ONE classification: 311 ÷ 600 ≈ 51.83%. That is what the table reports. We checked the rule at the original threshold and at both released sensitivity thresholds; the counts match.

Why matching the table is only the first check

The result says that we can recover these classifications from the released measurements. It does not independently validate the original model that described the clusters, the test texts it generated, or every assumption in the classification rule. It also does not directly refute a roughly 90% claim made for different models and a different definition of a job.

Two further examples show the difference between reproducing a number and supporting its interpretation.

The parent test: can a judge match a later group to an earlier record?

Suppose a neuron starts responding strongly to a new group of examples. The original judge sees that group and two earlier records: one from the same neuron and one from another neuron in the same layer. It tries to choose which earlier record belongs to the group. “Parent” is the name of this matching task; the choice does not establish a causal family tree.

The judge is correct about 81.16% of the time on the late-onset groups and about 92.42% on the controls. The study uses these rates to estimate how many groups might be unrelated to their earlier records. The calculation assumes that unrelated cases are guessed at 50% accuracy, and that related cases can be matched at the control accuracy.

Unrelated-share estimate(0.924167 − 0.811591) ÷ (0.924167 − 0.5) ≈ 0.2654

Under those assumptions, the estimate is about 26.54% of late-born groups. It is not 26.54% of all neurons.

The important question is whether the controls are a fair estimate of the judge’s ability on the real test items. Abay found that controls more often shared the marked token with the correct record, making them easier. His overlap-adjusted sensitivity changes the estimate and weakens the original interpretation, as shown below.

Is the main cluster oldest?

The observed fraction, 47.56%, reproduces. But Abay’s size/top-rank matched random groups reach 53.00%. The original rank-permutation arithmetic therefore does not establish the growth interpretation.

The main cluster is the one containing the highest-activation exemplar, not necessarily the largest by size. The better random-group reference is quoted from the released export.

Why the “main cluster is oldest” interpretation was withdrawn

The main cluster contains the highest-activation example at the final checkpoint. To date when a cluster appears, the original rule asks when its typical response first exceeds a high-response threshold and remains above it at the next saved checkpoint. This is an operational definition of onset, sometimes called birth.

The main cluster has the earliest onset in 47.56% of the eligible poles, counting ties. Initially, this looked high compared with a test that shuffled the existing onset dates. But that test did not reproduce how the clusters were constructed.

A stronger comparison forms random groups with the same sizes and keeps the top example in the main group. Those random groups reach 53.00%, above the real clusters. The original arithmetic still works, but it no longer establishes that a neuron’s main job tends to be its oldest. The lesson is practical: a convincing-looking statistic can depend on choosing the wrong reference comparison.

We reproduced the 47.56% count and original date-shuffling calculation. The 53.00% reference and the overlap-adjusted parent result are reported from Abay’s released outputs; we did not rerun those calculations from the unavailable raw banks. Both corrections are discussed in his updated account.

03 / Extend

Beyond the exact words.

The original examples raise a question that exact word matching cannot answer. Two passages can use different words while discussing similar ideas. We therefore compare both their wording and an embedding model’s representation of their meaning.

1. An embedding turns text into a list of numbers.

A sentence encoder is a separate model that maps a passage to a vector: a list of numbers designed to capture useful relationships between texts. Our primary encoder produces 384 numbers per context. We use the same encoder for every checkpoint, so we are not changing the measuring tool while comparing the examples.

Teaching example · not a measured result

“The battery stores energy.”
“This cell holds an electrical charge.”

These sentences share little exact wording but discuss related ideas. A text embedding can help detect that relationship. It is a model-based measure of meaning, not a guarantee that every relationship it finds is correct.

There are two different models in this analysis. OLMo is the large model whose neuron responses Abay measured. MiniLM is the smaller sentence encoder we use afterward to compare the recorded text. A MiniLM embedding is not an OLMo neuron’s internal activation vector. This distinction is essential to interpreting the result. The approach follows the sentence-embedding use of cosine described in Sentence-BERT.

2. Cosine asks whether two vectors point in the same direction.

Picture each vector as an arrow. Cosine similarity compares the angle between two arrows rather than their lengths. Its mathematical range is −1 to 1: 1 means the same direction, 0 means perpendicular directions, and −1 means opposite directions. For text embeddings, those geometric values are not universal labels for “same meaning,” “unrelated,” and “opposite meaning.” The encoder and the comparison set matter.

Optional arithmetic · a two-number teaching examplea = (1, 0)   b = (0.8, 0.6)

Both arrows have length 1. Their cosine is (1 × 0.8) + (0 × 0.6) = 0.8. Real context embeddings have 384 entries, but the same operation applies.

cosine(a, b) = (a · b) / (length(a) × length(b))

A cosine of 0.8 does not mean that 80% of the words overlap, that there is an 80% probability of a match, or that the neuron kept 80% of its function. It is a directional-similarity score.

3. A centroid summarizes the set of examples.

At each checkpoint, we have up to 20 context vectors for a pole. We first give each vector length 1, so a large original vector does not dominate simply because of its scale. We take their average, then rescale that average to length 1 as well. This summary direction is called a normalized centroid.

We then compare the centroid at step 560,000 with the same pole’s final centroid at step 1,413,814. Uniform weighting gives every exported context equal influence before the final normalization. This is a compact way to ask whether the two collections of examples occupy similar parts of the encoder’s text space. It also loses information: several different groups can average to a similar direction. We check that limitation later.

4. A control tells us what would count as surprisingly similar.

Many passages may resemble one another because they share general language, formatting, or a broad topic. A raw cosine cannot tell us whether a neuron has a distinctive pattern. We therefore compare its earlier examples with the final examples of other neurons in the same layer. These other-neuron examples are the control, also called the donor pool.

The question becomes: “Are the earlier examples closer to this neuron’s own final examples than to other available final examples from that layer?” Staying in the same layer makes this a more relevant comparison than picking arbitrary neurons from anywhere in the model. It still does not match every possible source of bias.

The harder test: remove repeated examples.

If the exact same passage appears in both checkpoints, it naturally makes them look similar. That could create an apparent continuity even without any broader pattern in the remaining examples. We therefore remove every shared full text from both sets and recompute the comparison.

This leaves 12–20 contexts in each of the same-neuron sets. We also prevent a query context from being reused as a control example and randomly choose control sets with the same number of target examples. We repeat that control subsampling 200 times. The aim is to compare like-sized sets rather than let one comparison benefit from a different amount of averaging.

The controlled difference becomes smaller, from +0.276 to +0.176, but remains positive in this analysis. That is the main new finding.

What does +0.176 mean in plain language?

For the eligible neurons, the earlier and final examples are more alike in the encoder’s text space than the earlier examples and the chosen other-neuron controls. This advantage persists after identical full texts are removed. It is evidence that exact example reuse does not explain the entire observed contrast.

It is a difference in cosine units, not a 17.6% improvement or a 17.6% probability. It does not tell us that every individual neuron is stable, or that any neuron performs the same computation throughout training. The estimate is an average for this particular exported sample.

How to read this figure

  1. Each row is a different analysis condition. “Original contexts” permits repeats. “No repeated contexts” removes shared full texts. Other rows change what text is encoded or which sentence encoder is used.
  2. The dot is the average controlled difference. Zero would mean that the same-neuron and other-neuron comparisons tie on average. A dot to the right of zero favors the same-neuron comparison.
  3. The horizontal line is a 95% bootstrap interval. It shows the range produced by the resampling procedure described next. It is not the range of all individual neurons’ scores and does not cover uncertainty from every possible source.
  4. The second view shows the trajectory through training. Select it in the dropdown. Semantic cosine compares embedded meaning; TF-IDF cosine compares weighted word and word-pair patterns; marked-token cosine compares the frequencies of the highlighted token strings; exact-context Jaccard compares repeated contexts. Each curve answers a different question, so do not interpret the vertical distance between different metrics as a statistical test.

Every curve reaches its self-comparison value at the final checkpoint. That endpoint is true by construction; it is not an additional discovery about learning.

TF-IDF stands for term frequency–inverse document frequency: count words and word pairs, but reduce the influence of terms common across many contexts. It measures lexical resemblance more directly than a sentence encoder does. Prefix only means using text only up to and including the marked token, so the comparison cannot rely on later text that was not yet available at that position.

The opening graph and the trajectory view resample the 24 underlying neurons for their shaded intervals. The controlled-difference view uses the nine-layer block procedure below. Those bands describe different samples and statistics.

Where does the interval [0.091, 0.293] come from?

If we had examined a different set of neurons, the average might differ. A bootstrap estimates one part of this sampling uncertainty by repeatedly drawing from the observations we already have and recalculating the statistic.

Here, some neurons share the same control donors because they are in the same layer. Treating all of those comparisons as independent could make uncertainty look too small. The follow-up therefore resamples whole layers as blocks, keeping the query-neuron contrasts in each selected layer together.

  1. Draw nine layers from the nine observed layers, allowing a layer to be drawn more than once.
  2. Keep all query-neuron comparisons inside each drawn layer, and calculate their overall mean.
  3. Repeat 10,000 times. Take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of those resampled means.

The resulting interval is an estimate conditional on these observations and this procedure. It is not a guarantee that a future model’s effect will fall inside the range. It does not remove selection bias, account for missing contexts, or create independent training runs. The exact recorded values and resampling units are available here.

Which neurons, encoders, and controls are included?

The export contains 41 poles from 40 neurons. Seventeen poles were selected using final outcomes. The primary analyses use the separate 24-pole random subset, which was sampled after excluding those 17. Of these 24, thirteen have another distinct exported neuron in the same layer.

The primary donor pool includes all eligible exported poles, including outcome-selected poles; the opposite pole of the same neuron is excluded. Restricting donors to the random subset leaves eight eligible neurons. This conditional sampling limits generalization.

The encoders are all-MiniLM-L6-v2 and paraphrase-MiniLM-L3-v2, both pinned to specific revisions. They are from the same family; agreement is a sensitivity check, not an independent replication.

04 / Challenge

A high cosine is
only half the comparison.

We next asked whether a simple text cue could produce the result. These are sensitivity checks: deliberate changes to the analysis that test how much the conclusion depends on a choice we made.

The masking experiment: a helpful-looking change can mislead.

To reduce the influence of the highlighted token, one version replaces it with the literal placeholder [MASK]. This changes the text we give to the sentence encoder. It does not rerun OLMo, remove the token from the original model’s input, or change the original activation measurement.

The raw same-neuron cosine rises from 0.598 to 0.830. Looking only at that number, we might wrongly conclude that removing the marked word makes meaning even more stable. But the other-neuron cosine also rises, from 0.322 to 0.702. Both sides receive the same placeholder, making a larger common component in the embeddings a plausible explanation.

The informative quantity is the difference: it falls from +0.276 to +0.128. This is why the control needs to appear beside the result, rather than being treated as a technical detail.

Remove the token without adding a placeholder.

We delete the marked token from the text, then remove shared full contexts. The difference is +0.178 [0.095, 0.294]. In another check, we exclude examples whose marked-token identities appear in both sets; that leaves +0.160 [0.079, 0.277].

These checks reduce particular shortcuts. They do not remove every occurrence of the word elsewhere in a passage, or every possible clue from topic and formatting.

Compare individual passages, not only averages.

A centroid can conceal a mixture of different topics. We therefore also compare individual passages across the two sets and average those pairwise cosines, before subtracting the control. That difference is +0.026 [0.012, 0.046].

This positive result suggests the finding is not solely caused by normalizing a diffuse average. Its smaller number belongs to a different statistic; it is not a percentage of the centroid effect.

16

Sixteen possible assignments. A real statistical limit.

The stricter random-only identity test contains four within-layer pairs: 2⁴ = 16 assignments. Its exact p = 0.0625 is the smallest attainable value, so it cannot reject at 5%. Requiring matching pole signs leaves only one pair, with p = 0.5.

The bootstrap and permutation analyses use different conditional comparisons. A positive bootstrap interval does not turn this exact test into a significant result.

What is that p-value actually saying?

A permutation test asks what would happen if the correct identity labels were not informative. In this test, we exchange the final-checkpoint identities within each same-layer pair, keeping the observed examples fixed. We compare the correctly matched score with every score obtainable under those exchanges.

Restricting both queries and controls to the random subset leaves eight eligible neurons arranged in four pairs. Each pair can keep or exchange its labels. There are only 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 total assignments. The observed matching is the most extreme, giving 1 ÷ 16 = 0.0625.

Even the strongest possible ordering in this design cannot give a p-value below 0.05. We therefore cannot claim that this exact test rejects its null at the conventional 5% threshold. A p-value of 0.0625 is not a 6.25% probability that the hypothesis is false; it describes this statistic under the particular random-label comparison.

Why does that coexist with a positive bootstrap interval?

The two analyses are not interchangeable. The follow-up interval uses thirteen query neurons, donors from all eligible exported poles, and resampling across nine layers. The stricter identity test uses eight neurons and random-subset donors only. They ask different conditional questions with different amounts of information.

The honest reading is: several controlled comparisons are positive in the released sample, but the stricter exact test is too coarse to cross 5%. A larger, independently selected sample with more neurons in each layer is needed for a stronger confirmation.

05 / Boundaries

What this can tell us.
What it cannot.

Supported within this sample

Some semantic continuity in the text.

Changing examples and activation magnitudes coexist with a positive same-neuron semantic contrast. The contrast persists under repeated-context removal, token controls, prefix-only input, and a related second encoder.

Still an open question

The neuron’s causal role.

Sentence embeddings describe text. They are not the model’s activation vectors. Similar meaning does not establish a single job, causal ancestry, or the same downstream computation.

“Responds to” and “is responsible for” are different claims.

If a neuron activates during battery-related passages, we know there is an association in those examples. We do not yet know what contribution that neuron makes to the model’s next-token prediction. Other units may carry similar information, and the connections using this neuron’s output may change during training.

A stronger test would deliberately change the neuron’s contribution—for example, set its activation to zero at chosen positions—and measure how the model’s predictions change. That is a causal intervention. To establish continuity of a causal role, we would need to compare the consequences of well-matched interventions across checkpoints, not only the meanings of the activating text.

Why call the new analysis exploratory?

We saw the published data and headlines before designing this extension. After the initial similarity result, we designed the repeated-context and token-removal follow-ups on the same dataset. Those checks are useful, but the result also helped choose the questions. A confirmatory study would fix the test in advance and evaluate it on a fresh sample that did not influence those choices.

Similarly, a held-out set means observations kept aside while methods or thresholds are chosen. They are examined for evaluation afterward. Splitting thousands of text snippets from the same few neurons is not the same as holding out entire neurons or independently trained models.

  • One training run

    Ten thousand bootstrap draws are not ten thousand independently trained models.

  • A selected export

    Top-20 contexts are truncated and change across checkpoints. The 24-pole random subset is conditional on excluding outcome-selected examples.

  • Exploratory follow-up

    The disjoint-context plan was written after seeing the first similarity result. It uses the same data, not a held-out confirmation sample.

  • Missing raw banks

    The tested public S3 object returned HTTP 403. Full activations, judge-visible records, and aligned interventions are needed for stronger tests.
